The Nuclear Fuel Cycle

A GIS story that follows nuclear fuel from uranium extraction to conversion, enrichment, fabrication, reactor use, spent-fuel management and geological disposal. A reactor is only one node in a much wider industrial geography.

Read the story from top to bottom. At each stage, the button opens that part of the nuclear geography on the atlas. On mobile this replaces the former control dashboard with the same simple interaction used by the narrative stories.
Stage 1 · Extraction

The fuel cycle begins underground

Before a reactor can produce heat, uranium must first be recovered from a geological deposit. The extraction layer shows mining sites where uranium-bearing material enters the industrial chain.

Modern uranium production can include in-situ recovery, where chemical solutions circulate through the ore body and bring dissolved uranium to the surface.

The location of the mine already shows that the nuclear fuel cycle is international: material can travel thousands of kilometres before it ever reaches a reactor.

Stage 2 · Conversion

From concentrate to uranium hexafluoride

Mining does not produce reactor fuel. Uranium concentrates must first be purified and chemically converted. In the European chain shown here, Malvési represents conversion toward UF4 and Tricastin the subsequent production of UF6.

UF6 matters because it can be handled as a gas in centrifuge enrichment plants. Conversion is therefore the chemical bridge between mining and isotope separation.

Stage 3 · Enrichment

Changing the isotopic composition

Natural uranium contains too little U-235 for most light-water power reactors. Centrifuge plants separate isotopes and increase the U-235 fraction in the product stream.

The map reveals that enrichment is a distinct industrial stage, geographically separated from both mining and fuel fabrication.

03_uranium_enrichment Technology: gas centrifuges
Stage 4 · Fuel fabrication

Uranium becomes a reactor component

Enriched uranium is still not a fuel assembly. Fuel factories transform uranium compounds into ceramic fuel, pellets, rods and finally assemblies engineered for a specific reactor design.

Fuel geometry, cladding and assembly design are reactor-specific, linking nuclear material directly to reactor engineering.

04_fuel_fabrication Pellets · rods · assemblies
Stage 5 · Reactor use

The reactor is the middle of the chain, not the beginning

Only now does the fuel enter the reactor. Fission produces heat and electricity, while the isotopic and chemical composition of the fuel changes continuously under irradiation.

The Swiss power-reactor layer also distinguishes operating sites from a plant already in decommissioning.

After irradiation, the fuel leaves the reactor with a radically different isotopic inventory and becomes the input to the back end of the fuel cycle.

09_nuclear_power_switzerland Irradiation and fission
Stage 6 · Reprocessing

Spent fuel can become an industrial feedstock again

At La Hague, spent fuel can be treated chemically. Uranium and plutonium are separated from highly radioactive fission products, allowing selected materials to re-enter the fuel cycle.

Reprocessing changes the boundary between resource and waste: some materials are recovered, while others enter dedicated waste-management streams.

05_reprocessing_recycling Example: La Hague
Stage 7 · Interim storage

Waste management is a chain of its own

Spent fuel and radioactive waste require controlled storage before final disposal or further treatment. Switzerland is especially useful here because the map distinguishes central storage at ZWILAG from on-site storage at nuclear plants.

Interim storage is not the same thing as a geological repository. Storage provides time for cooling, shielding and institutional control; it is an engineered waiting stage, not the final destination.

Stage 8 · Research

Nuclear infrastructure is not only power generation

Research facilities form another branch of nuclear geography. PSI hosts neutron facilities and laboratories for highly radioactive materials, while EPFL's CROCUS is a very-low-power teaching and experimental reactor.

These sites explain why a nuclear map must distinguish power plants, research reactors and laboratories.

08_nuclear_research PSI · CROCUS
Stage 9 · Geological disposal

The final stage moves underground again

The end of the chain is not a surface warehouse. Geological repositories are designed to isolate long-lived radioactive material deep underground using engineered and geological barriers.

ONKALO, Forsmark and Switzerland's Nördlich Lägern project show three European approaches at different implementation stages.

The repository closes the map by returning the material underground, now inside a deliberately engineered system designed for isolation over very long timescales.
